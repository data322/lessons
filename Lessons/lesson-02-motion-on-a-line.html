1   Overview

How fast is a ball rolling? Where will a car be in five seconds? How quickly does a sprinter accelerate out of the blocks? All of these questions are answered by kinematics — the branch of mechanics that describes how objects move without asking why they move. This lesson builds kinematics from the ground up for motion along a single straight line, which physicists call one-dimensional motion.

What you will learn: How to define position, displacement, distance, average velocity, and average speed precisely; how to read and interpret position–time graphs; how instantaneous velocity emerges from the limit of an average; what acceleration means; how position, velocity, and acceleration are related by derivatives and integrals; and how to solve problems under constant acceleration using the four kinematic equations.

We treat motion rigorously using calculus — the natural language of change — so definitions here are exact, not approximate. If you are comfortable with limits and derivatives you will find the ideas fall into place cleanly. If calculus is new to you, focus on the geometric interpretations (slopes and areas) which give the same answers with less formalism.

Notation convention used in this lesson: We restrict motion to a single axis and choose a positive direction at the start of each problem. Positive quantities point in that direction; negative quantities point opposite. This sign convention is the backbone of everything that follows.

2   Coordinate System & Position

Before we can discuss motion we need a reference: a fixed point from which to measure distances and a positive direction along the line.

The number line as a coordinate axis

Choose a point on the line and call it the origin, labeled \(O\) or \(x = 0\). Designate one direction as positive — conventional choices are "right," "up," or "east," but any consistent choice works. The opposite direction is negative.

Figure 2.1 — A number line coordinate axis. Position \(x = +2\ \text{m}\) means 2 metres in the positive direction from the origin.

Position

The position of an object, \(x\), is its signed distance from the origin along the axis. It is a scalar in 1-D (because direction is encoded by the sign) and its SI unit is the metre (m).

Because the object can move, position is really a function of time:

\[x = x(t)\]

The complete function \(x(t)\) is called the object's position function or equation of motion. Knowing \(x(t)\) tells us everything about the object's location at every instant.

Sign ≠ direction of motion. A negative position \(x < 0\) does not mean the object is moving in the negative direction; it means the object is on the negative side of the origin. The direction of motion is determined by the sign of velocity, not position.

3   Displacement

We rarely care about a single snapshot of position; we care about change in position.

Definition

The displacement \(\Delta x\) of an object over a time interval \([t_1,\, t_2]\) is:

\[\Delta x = x(t_2) - x(t_1) = x_2 - x_1\]

Definition — Displacement (1-D)

Displacement is a signed scalar in 1-D: positive means the object ended up further in the positive direction than it started; negative means the opposite.

Figure 3.1 — Displacement is the net change in position: \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1 = 4 - 1 = +3\ \text{m}\).

Worked Example 3.1 — Computing Displacement

A jogger starts at \(x_1 = +8\ \text{m}\) and ends at \(x_2 = +3\ \text{m}\). Find the displacement.

1 Apply the definition: \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1 = 3 - 8 = -5\ \text{m}\).
2 The displacement is \(-5\ \text{m}\): the jogger moved 5 metres in the negative direction even though both positions are positive.

Displacement in three dimensions

In 2-D or 3-D, displacement becomes a true vector: \(\Delta\vec{r} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1\). For this lesson we confine ourselves to 1-D, where the signed scalar \(\Delta x\) suffices.

4   Distance Travelled

Displacement asks "where did you end up relative to where you started?" Distance asks "how many metres did your odometer click?" They are related but distinct.

Definition

The distance travelled (or path length) \(d\) is the total length of the path traced by the object, regardless of direction:

\[d = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)|\, dt\]

Definition — Distance Travelled

In plain terms: add up all the metres traveled, counting backtracking as additional distance. Distance is always non-negative.

Figure 4.1 — Object travels +5 m then −3 m. Displacement = +2 m; distance = 5 + 3 = 8 m.

Displacement ≠ Distance. Displacement = \(\Delta x = +2\ \text{m}\). Distance = \(d = 8\ \text{m}\). They are equal only when the object never reverses direction. Confusing them is one of the most common errors in introductory physics.

Worked Example 4.1 — Distance vs. Displacement

A particle moves along the x-axis: \(x(t) = t^3 - 3t\) metres for \(0 \le t \le 3\ \text{s}\).

1 Find the velocity: \(v(t) = x'(t) = 3t^2 - 3 = 3(t-1)(t+1)\). Velocity changes sign at \(t = 1\ \text{s}\).
2 Evaluate positions at key times: \(x(0) = 0\), \(x(1) = 1 - 3 = -2\ \text{m}\), \(x(3) = 27 - 9 = 18\ \text{m}\).
3 Displacement: \(\Delta x = x(3) - x(0) = 18 - 0 = +18\ \text{m}\).
4 Distance: leg 1 \([0,1]\): \(|-2 - 0| = 2\ \text{m}\). Leg 2 \([1,3]\): \(|18 - (-2)| = 20\ \text{m}\). Total: \(d = 22\ \text{m}\).

5   Average Velocity & Average Speed

Average velocity

The average velocity over a time interval \([t_1, t_2]\) is the displacement divided by the elapsed time:

\[\bar{v} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}\]

Definition — Average Velocity

Average velocity is a signed scalar in 1-D, with SI unit m/s. A positive \(\bar{v}\) means the net motion was in the positive direction; negative means the opposite.

Average speed

Average speed uses distance rather than displacement:

\[\bar{s} = \frac{d}{\Delta t} = \frac{\text{total distance travelled}}{\text{elapsed time}}\]

Definition — Average Speed

Average speed is always non-negative. It equals \(|\bar{v}|\) only if the object never reverses.

Worked Example 5.1 — Average Velocity and Speed

A car drives 60 km east in 1 h, then 20 km west in 0.5 h. Take east as positive.

1 Displacement: \(\Delta x = +60 + (-20) = +40\ \text{km}\).
2 Distance: \(d = 60 + 20 = 80\ \text{km}\).
3 Total time: \(\Delta t = 1.5\ \text{h}\).
4 Average velocity: \(\bar{v} = 40/1.5 \approx +26.7\ \text{km/h}\) (eastward).
5 Average speed: \(\bar{s} = 80/1.5 \approx 53.3\ \text{km/h}\). Note \(\bar{s} \neq |\bar{v}|\).

6   The Position–Time Graph

A graph of \(x\) versus \(t\) encodes the entire motion. Learning to read it fluently is one of the most important skills in kinematics.

Slope = average velocity

The slope of the secant line joining two points \((t_1, x_1)\) and \((t_2, x_2)\) on the \(x\)–\(t\) graph is exactly the average velocity:

\[\bar{v} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \text{slope of secant}\]

Figure 6.1 — Left: constant-velocity motion (\(x = 1.5t\)) — straight line, constant slope. Right: parabolic motion (\(x = -t^2 + 4t + 1\)) — slope (velocity) varies; zero at the peak.

Reading the graph

  • Steep slope → large \(|\bar{v}|\); the object is moving fast.
  • Flat (horizontal) segment → \(\bar{v} = 0\); the object is at rest.
  • Positive slope → moving in the positive direction.
  • Negative slope → moving in the negative direction.
  • Slope changes sign → object reversed direction at that instant.

7   Instantaneous Velocity

Average velocity describes an interval. To describe motion at a single moment we must shrink the interval to zero — this is where calculus enters.

The limiting process

Fix a time \(t\) and consider a second time \(t + \Delta t\). The average velocity over \([t,\, t+\Delta t]\) is \(\dfrac{x(t+\Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t}\). As \(\Delta t \to 0\) the secant line through \((t, x(t))\) and \((t+\Delta t, x(t+\Delta t))\) rotates until it becomes the tangent line to the curve at \((t, x(t))\). The slope of that tangent line is the instantaneous velocity:

\[v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{x(t+\Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{dx}{dt}\]

Definition — Instantaneous Velocity

Instantaneous velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time. It is a signed scalar with SI unit m/s.

Figure 7.1 — As \(\Delta t \to 0\) the secant lines (gold) rotate onto the tangent line (red). The slope of the tangent is the instantaneous velocity at point \(P\).

Worked Example 7.1 — Instantaneous Velocity from a Position Function

A particle moves as \(x(t) = 3t^2 - 12t + 7\ \text{m}\). Find \(v(t)\) and evaluate at \(t = 1\ \text{s}\) and \(t = 3\ \text{s}\).

1 Differentiate: \(v(t) = \dfrac{dx}{dt} = 6t - 12\ \text{m/s}\).
2 At \(t = 1\ \text{s}\): \(v(1) = 6(1) - 12 = -6\ \text{m/s}\). Moving in the negative direction.
3 At \(t = 3\ \text{s}\): \(v(3) = 6(3) - 12 = +6\ \text{m/s}\). Moving in the positive direction.
4 The object was momentarily at rest when \(v = 0\): \(6t - 12 = 0 \Rightarrow t = 2\ \text{s}\).
Velocity as a rate of change. Velocity is the instantaneous rate at which position changes. Every time you see \(dx/dt\) in physics you are asking: "How fast and in what direction is \(x\) changing right now?" The answer is velocity.

8   Instantaneous Speed

Instantaneous speed is the magnitude of instantaneous velocity — it strips the sign and keeps only how fast the object is moving:

\[\text{speed} = |v(t)| = \left|\frac{dx}{dt}\right|\]

Speed is always non-negative and has no directional information. A speedometer reads speed, not velocity. In Example 7.1 above:

  • At \(t = 1\ \text{s}\): speed = \(|-6| = 6\ \text{m/s}\).
  • At \(t = 3\ \text{s}\): speed = \(|+6| = 6\ \text{m/s}\).
  • At \(t = 2\ \text{s}\): speed = \(0\ \text{m/s}\) (momentarily stopped).
Speed < 0 is undefined. If your calculation gives a negative speed you have computed a signed velocity and must take its absolute value, or there is an error in your work.

9   Acceleration

Velocity can itself change. The rate of change of velocity is acceleration.

Average acceleration

\[\bar{a} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}\]

Average Acceleration

Instantaneous acceleration

\[a(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{v(t+\Delta t) - v(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2}\]

Instantaneous Acceleration (second derivative of position)

The SI unit of acceleration is m/s² (metres per second per second). Acceleration is signed: positive acceleration in the positive direction; negative acceleration (sometimes called deceleration in everyday speech) in the negative direction.

"Deceleration" is ambiguous. "Decelerating" means slowing down, which happens when velocity and acceleration have opposite signs. An object moving in the negative direction with a positive acceleration is slowing down — yet acceleration is positive. Always use the sign of \(a\) explicitly; avoid "deceleration" in formal work.

Worked Example 9.1 — Acceleration from Velocity

A particle has velocity \(v(t) = 4t^2 - 8t\ \text{m/s}\). Find the acceleration at \(t = 3\ \text{s}\) and determine whether the particle is speeding up or slowing down.

1 \(a(t) = dv/dt = 8t - 8\ \text{m/s}^2\). At \(t = 3\): \(a = 8(3)-8 = +16\ \text{m/s}^2\).
2 \(v(3) = 4(9) - 8(3) = 36 - 24 = +12\ \text{m/s}\).
3 \(v\) and \(a\) are both positive — same sign — so the particle is speeding up.
Speeding up vs. slowing down. An object speeds up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign, and slows down when they have opposite signs. Zero velocity with nonzero acceleration means the object is about to reverse direction.

10   The \(x\)–\(v\)–\(a\) Hierarchy

Position, velocity, and acceleration are related through differentiation and integration. This is the master diagram of kinematics:

\[x(t) \xrightarrow{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}} v(t) \xrightarrow{\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}} a(t)\] \[a(t) \xrightarrow{\displaystyle\int dt} v(t) \xrightarrow{\displaystyle\int dt} x(t)\]

Figure 10.1 — The \(x\)–\(v\)–\(a\) hierarchy for \(x = t^2\), \(v = 2t\), \(a = 2\ \text{m/s}^2\). Each panel is the derivative of the one above and the integral of the one below.

The three panels are linked: the slope of the \(x\)–\(t\) curve at any moment equals the value of \(v\) at that moment; the slope of the \(v\)–\(t\) line equals \(a\). Conversely, the area under the \(a\)–\(t\) curve gives \(\Delta v\), and the area under the \(v\)–\(t\) curve gives \(\Delta x\).

11   Area Under Curves

Derivatives move down the hierarchy (\(x \to v \to a\)); integrals move up (\(a \to v \to x\)).

Area under \(v\)–\(t\) = displacement

\[\Delta x = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\, dt\]

The signed area between the \(v\)–\(t\) curve and the time axis equals the displacement. Area above the axis (\(v > 0\)) is positive displacement; area below (\(v < 0\)) is negative displacement. The total area (using \(|v|\)) gives distance.

Area under \(a\)–\(t\) = change in velocity

\[\Delta v = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a(t)\, dt\]

Figure 11.1 — The signed area under the \(v\)–\(t\) curve equals displacement. Red region = positive displacement; blue region = negative displacement.

Worked Example 11.1 — Displacement from a v–t Graph

From the graph above, \(v(t) = -t + 3\ \text{m/s}\) for \(0 \le t \le 5\ \text{s}\). Find displacement and distance.

1 Velocity is zero at \(t = 3\ \text{s}\).
2 Displacement: \(\Delta x = \int_0^5 (-t+3)\,dt = \left[-\tfrac{t^2}{2}+3t\right]_0^5 = (-12.5+15) = 2.5\ \text{m}\).
3 Distance: \(d = \int_0^3(3-t)\,dt + \int_3^5(t-3)\,dt = 4.5 + 2 = 6.5\ \text{m}\).
Where does \(\tfrac{1}{2}at^2\) come from geometrically? The area of the velocity triangle is exactly \(\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot T\cdot aT = \tfrac{1}{2}aT^2\). This geometric fact — that a uniformly accelerating body covers the same distance as one moving at the mean speed for the same time — was proved in 14th-century Oxford and Paris, three centuries before Newton. Explore the history and the proof in Side Quest A: The Mean Speed Theorem.

12   Constant Acceleration

When acceleration \(a\) is constant, the integrals are elementary and yield the four kinematic equations. These are the workhorses of introductory mechanics.

Derivation

Start with \(a(t) = a = \text{const}\). Integrate once:

\[v(t) = v_0 + at \tag{1}\]

Integrate again, with initial position \(x_0 = x(0)\):

\[x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 \tag{2}\]

Eliminate \(t\) between (1) and (2) (solve (1) for \(t\), substitute):

\[v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,(x - x_0) \tag{3}\]

Average (1) at \(t=0\) and \(t\), then integrate:

\[x = x_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)\,t \tag{4}\]

Derivation without calculus

The same four equations follow from definitions alone — no integration required. This is how they can be derived by anyone who knows algebra and the mean-speed result.

Step 1 — Equation (1) from the definition of constant acceleration. If \(a\) is constant, then \(a = \Delta v / \Delta t = (v - v_0)/t\). Multiply both sides by \(t\) and rearrange:

\[ v = v_0 + at \tag{1} \]

Step 2 — Equation (4) from the mean-speed theorem (§11). For any linearly changing velocity, the mean velocity equals the average of the initial and final values, and displacement equals mean velocity times time:

\[ \Delta x = \bar{v}\,t = \tfrac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t \tag{4} \]

Equations (1) and (4) are the two primitive results — one from the definition of acceleration, one from the geometry of the \(v\)–\(t\) graph. Equations (2) and (3) follow by substitution.

Step 3 — Equation (2) by substituting (1) into (4). Replace \(v\) in (4) with \(v_0 + at\):

\[ \Delta x = \tfrac{v_0 + (v_0 + at)}{2}\,t = \tfrac{2v_0 + at}{2}\,t = v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 \tag{2} \]

Step 4 — Equation (3) by eliminating \(t\) between (1) and (4). From (1): \(t = (v - v_0)/a\). Substitute into (4):

\[ \Delta x = \tfrac{v_0 + v}{2} \cdot \tfrac{v - v_0}{a} = \tfrac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2a} \]

Rearrange:

\[ v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x \tag{3} \]
The ½ is not a coincidence. It appears in equations (2) and (4) because the mean of a linearly varying quantity is exactly halfway between its endpoints — which is the area of a triangle being half the enclosing rectangle. See Side Quest A: The Mean Speed Theorem for the full geometric proof.

Strategy for constant-acceleration problems: List the five variables (\(x_0, x, v_0, v, a, t\) — only five independent ones since \(x_0\) is usually set to zero). Identify three known quantities and one unknown. Choose the equation that contains exactly those four variables.

Worked Example 12.1 — Braking Car

A car traveling at \(v_0 = 30\ \text{m/s}\) brakes uniformly to rest. It covers \(d = 90\ \text{m}\) while stopping. Find the braking acceleration and the time to stop.

1 Known: \(v_0 = 30\ \text{m/s}\), \(v = 0\), \(\Delta x = 90\ \text{m}\). Unknown: \(a\). Missing: \(t\). Use (3).
2 \(0 = (30)^2 + 2a(90) \Rightarrow a = -\dfrac{900}{180} = -5\ \text{m/s}^2\).
3 Now find \(t\). Use (1): \(0 = 30 + (-5)t \Rightarrow t = 6\ \text{s}\).
4 The braking acceleration is \(-5\ \text{m/s}^2\) (opposing the motion) and stopping takes 6 s.

Worked Example 12.2 — Free Fall

Near Earth's surface, a freely-falling object has constant downward acceleration \(g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\). A ball is dropped from rest at height \(h = 45\ \text{m}\). Take upward as positive, so \(a = -g = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\), \(x_0 = 45\ \text{m}\), \(v_0 = 0\). Find the time and speed at impact.

1 At impact: \(x = 0\). Use (2): \(0 = 45 + 0 \cdot t + \tfrac{1}{2}(-9.8)t^2\).
2 \(4.9t^2 = 45 \Rightarrow t = \sqrt{45/4.9} \approx 3.03\ \text{s}\).
3 Speed at impact: \(|v| = |v_0 + at| = |0 + (-9.8)(3.03)| \approx 29.7\ \text{m/s}\).

Worked Example 12.3 — Ball Thrown Upward

A ball is thrown straight up at \(v_0 = 20\ \text{m/s}\) from \(x_0 = 0\), \(a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\). Find (a) the maximum height, (b) the time to reach it, and (c) the speed when it returns to \(x = 0\).

1 (a) Max height: \(v = 0\) there. Use (3): \(0 = 400 + 2(-9.8)x_{\max} \Rightarrow x_{\max} = 400/19.6 \approx 20.4\ \text{m}\).
2 (b) Time to peak: \(0 = 20 - 9.8t \Rightarrow t = 20/9.8 \approx 2.04\ \text{s}\).
3 (c) Returns to \(x = 0\): by symmetry or use (3) with \(x = 0\): \(v^2 = 400 \Rightarrow |v| = 20\ \text{m/s}\). Speed equals launch speed — no energy lost.

Worked Example 12.4 — Using Equation (4) when a is unknown

A cyclist speeds up uniformly from \(v_0 = 4\ \text{m/s}\) to \(v = 12\ \text{m/s}\) in \(t = 5\ \text{s}\). Find the displacement. The acceleration is not given and is not needed.

1 Known: \(v_0 = 4\ \text{m/s}\), \(v = 12\ \text{m/s}\), \(t = 5\ \text{s}\). Unknown: \(\Delta x\). Missing variable: \(a\). Choose equation (4).
2 \(\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)\,t = \tfrac{1}{2}(4 + 12)(5) = \tfrac{1}{2}(16)(5) = 40\ \text{m}\).
3 Check via (1) and (2): \(a = (v-v_0)/t = 8/5 = 1.6\ \text{m/s}^2\). Then \(\Delta x = v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = 4(5) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1.6)(25) = 20 + 20 = 40\ \text{m}\ \checkmark\)
Did Galileo discover these equations? The kinematic equations describe the mathematics of constant acceleration. Galileo's great achievement was to establish experimentally — using inclined planes and careful timing — that freely falling bodies actually undergo this kind of motion. He did it by confirming the law of odd numbers (\(1:3:5:7:\ldots\)) and the law of squares (\(x \propto t^2\)). Explore the history in Side Quest B: Galileo and Falling Bodies.

13   Summary

Here is the complete conceptual map of 1-D kinematics in one place.

QuantitySymbolDefinitionUnitSigned?
Position\(x\)Signed distance from originmYes
Displacement\(\Delta x\)\(x_2 - x_1\)mYes
Distance\(d\)\(\int |v|\,dt\)mNo (≥0)

Key relationships

  • \(v(t) = dx/dt\) — velocity is the derivative of position.
  • \(a(t) = dv/dt\) — acceleration is the derivative of velocity.
  • \(\Delta x = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v\,dt\) — area under \(v\)–\(t\) is displacement.
  • \(\Delta v = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a\,dt\) — area under \(a\)–\(t\) is change in velocity.
  • Slope of \(x\)–\(t\) = \(v\); slope of \(v\)–\(t\) = \(a\).
  • Object speeds up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign; slows down when opposite.

Four kinematic equations (constant \(a\) only)

\[\begin{aligned} v &= v_0 + at \\ x &= x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 \\ v^2 &= v_0^2 + 2a(x-x_0) \\ x &= x_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0+v)t \end{aligned}\]

Glossary

Position, \(x\)
The signed distance of an object from the chosen origin along the coordinate axis. A function of time: \(x = x(t)\). SI unit: metre (m).
Position function
The complete function \(x(t)\) describing an object's location at every instant. Also called the equation of motion.
Displacement, \(\Delta x\)
The signed change in position: \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1\). Positive if the final position is further in the positive direction than the initial; negative otherwise. SI unit: m.
Distance travelled, \(d\)
The total path length traversed by the object, regardless of direction: \(d = \int|v|\,dt\). Always non-negative. Equal to \(|\Delta x|\) only if the object never reverses. SI unit: m.
Average velocity, \(\bar{v}\)
Displacement divided by elapsed time: \(\bar{v} = \Delta x / \Delta t\). Signed; equal to the slope of the secant line on the \(x\)–\(t\) graph. SI unit: m/s.
Average speed, \(\bar{s}\)
Distance travelled divided by elapsed time: \(\bar{s} = d / \Delta t\). Always non-negative. Equal to \(|\bar{v}|\) only if the object never reverses. SI unit: m/s.
Instantaneous velocity, \(v(t)\)
The limit of average velocity as the time interval shrinks to zero: \(v = dx/dt\). Equals the slope of the tangent to the \(x\)–\(t\) graph. Signed. SI unit: m/s.
Instantaneous speed
The magnitude of instantaneous velocity: \(|v(t)|\). Always non-negative; carries no directional information. SI unit: m/s.
Acceleration, \(a(t)\)
The instantaneous rate of change of velocity: \(a = dv/dt = d^2x/dt^2\). Signed; equals the slope of the tangent to the \(v\)–\(t\) graph. SI unit: m/s².
Kinematic equations
Four algebraic relations among \(x_0, x, v_0, v, a, t\) that are valid only when acceleration is constant: \(v = v_0+at\), \(x = x_0+v_0t+\tfrac{1}{2}at^2\), \(v^2 = v_0^2+2a\Delta x\), \(x = x_0+\tfrac{1}{2}(v_0+v)t\).
Secant line
A straight line that passes through two points on a curve. The slope of the secant on the \(x\)–\(t\) graph equals average velocity over the corresponding time interval.
Tangent line
The limiting secant as the two points merge. Its slope at any point on the \(x\)–\(t\) graph equals the instantaneous velocity at that moment.
Free fall
Motion under gravity alone, with constant downward acceleration \(g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\) near Earth's surface. Air resistance is neglected.
