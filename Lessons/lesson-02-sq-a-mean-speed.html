A1   Overview

A side quest is a detour that rewards curiosity. You have already mastered the language of kinematics — position, velocity, acceleration, the \(v\)–\(t\) graph. This side quest uses that language to visit one of the most remarkable achievements of medieval science: a geometric proof, made in Oxford and Paris around 1335–1360, that the distance covered by a uniformly accelerating body equals the distance it would cover at its mean speed.

That statement is not merely a curiosity. It is the mean speed theorem — and it is the geometric heart of the kinematic equation \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\). Galileo built his description of free fall on it. The equation you have been using comes, ultimately, from 14th-century geometry.

Prerequisites. This side quest assumes you are comfortable with Lesson 2's vocabulary: displacement, average velocity, the \(v\)–\(t\) graph, and the idea that the area under a \(v\)–\(t\) curve equals displacement. No calculus is required for the geometric proofs in §A5–A6; §A7–A8 translate everything into the calculus language of Lesson 2.
What you will see. Who proved the mean speed theorem and how; the medieval language they used; Oresme's geometric proof using triangles and rectangles; the visual "hump and notch" rearrangement argument; how it all becomes \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\); and why it matters that this was known three centuries before Newton.

A2   The Merton Calculators

In the first half of the fourteenth century, a group of scholars at Merton College, Oxford, launched a systematic attempt to apply mathematics to Aristotelian natural philosophy. They are known today as the Oxford Calculators or the Merton School. Their key figures:

  • Thomas Bradwardine (c. 1300–1349) — later Archbishop of Canterbury; established a mathematical law of motion and set the tone for the group's quantitative approach.
  • William Heytesbury (c. 1313–1372) — in his Regule solvendi sophismata (1335), stated and proved the mean speed theorem as a rule for calculating the distance traversed under uniform acceleration.
  • Richard Swineshead (fl. 1340–1354) — so dominant a mathematician that he was called the Calculator; his Liber Calculationum extended the Oxford methods across every measurable quality.
  • John Dumbleton (fl. 1331–1349) — provided physical interpretations and a more encyclopedic treatment in his Summa logicae et philosophiae naturalis.

The Merton group proved the theorem in the language of intension and remission of forms — the scholastic framework for how qualities increase or decrease. Their proofs were verbal and proto-algebraic, not geometric.

The geometric proof came from Paris. Nicole Oresme (c. 1323–1382), a Norman scholar attached to the Collège de Navarre, invented a way to draw pictures of how qualities vary — what he called the latitude of forms. Applied to motion, this produced a velocity–time diagram indistinguishable in concept from the ones you drew in Lesson 2. On that diagram, the mean speed theorem becomes a statement about the equality of two areas.

Why Oxford and Paris? Both cities housed Europe's leading universities. Oxford's strength was logic and mathematics; Paris's was theology and natural philosophy. The two traditions cross-pollinated steadily through traveling scholars, and Oresme knew the Merton results before he supplied the geometric proof.

A3   The Language of Motion

The Oxford Calculators did not have the word "acceleration." They worked within Aristotle's framework of qualities — properties that objects possess to varying degrees — and used a specialized Latin vocabulary to describe how qualities change. Before reading their results, it helps to build a translation dictionary.

Medieval Latin Literal meaning Modern equivalent
motus uniformis uniform motion constant velocity
motus difformis difform motion any non-constant velocity
motus uniformiter difformis uniformly difform motion constant acceleration (velocity changes at a constant rate)
gradus velocitatis degree of velocity instantaneous velocity
latitudo formae latitude of the form value of a quality at a given instant (height on Oresme's diagram)
longitudo longitude the time axis (horizontal extent) on Oresme's diagram
intensio / remissio intension / remission increase / decrease of a quality; positive / negative acceleration
medium gradum middle degree mean (average) velocity: \(\bar{v} = (v_0 + v_f)/2\)

The key term is uniformiter difformis: "uniformly difform." A uniformly difform velocity is one that changes at a constant rate — exactly what we mean by constant acceleration. The Merton theorem concerns motion that starts at one gradus velocitatis and ends at another, with the rate of change constant throughout.

A subtle word: "difform." In modern English "uniform" means constant. In the medieval framework, uniformis also means constant, but difformis means non-constant. A quality that is uniformiter difformis is therefore non-constant in a uniform way — changing at a constant rate. The "uniformly" applies to the rate of change, not the value itself.

A4   The Theorem

The mean speed theorem in three registers:

Heytesbury's formulation (c. 1335)

"Of every non-uniform or difform quality uniformly difform from no degree to some degree, the measure, and the quantity of the whole, is that of the mean degree."

Plain English

A body that accelerates uniformly — starting from rest and reaching final speed \(v_f\) — covers the same distance in time \(T\) as a body that moves at constant speed \(\tfrac{1}{2}v_f\) for the same time. More generally (with non-zero initial speed \(v_0\)), the constant speed that produces the same displacement is the arithmetic mean \(\bar{v} = (v_0 + v_f)/2\).

Modern notation

\[ \Delta x \;=\; \bar{v}\cdot T, \qquad \bar{v} = \frac{v_0 + v_f}{2} \qquad \bigl(\text{valid when acceleration is constant}\bigr) \]
This is already in Lesson 2. Kinematic equation (4) from Lesson 2 is \(x = x_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)\,t\). That is precisely the mean speed theorem: displacement equals mean speed times time. The Oxford Calculators proved this in 1335; Newton's Principia appeared in 1687.

A5   Oresme's Geometric Proof

Around 1350, Nicole Oresme published his Tractatus de configurationibus qualitatum et motuum. In it, he introduced the idea of drawing a quality as a vertical height above a horizontal "longitude." The resulting shape — he called it the figura or configuratio — has an area, and Oresme argued that the area represents the total quantity of the quality over the interval.

For motion, the quality is velocity and the longitude is time. Oresme's diagram is precisely our \(v\)–\(t\) graph: velocity vertical, time horizontal. The area enclosed — which he called the quantitas motus — is the displacement. This is the geometric meaning of \(\Delta x = \int v\,dt\), stated without calculus.

The triangle and the rectangle

For uniformiter difformis motion starting from rest, the velocity–time figure is a right triangle: a straight line rising from zero at \(t = 0\) to \(v_f\) at \(t = T\), closing against the time axis. For motion at the constant mean speed \(\bar{v} = v_f/2\), the figure is a rectangle of height \(v_f/2\) and width \(T\).

These two figures have identical areas:

\[ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot T \cdot v_f}_{\text{triangle (accelerating)}} \;=\; \underbrace{T \cdot \tfrac{v_f}{2}}_{\text{rectangle (mean speed)}} \]

Therefore — since area equals displacement — the two motions produce the same displacement in time \(T\). The mean speed theorem is proved.

Figure A5.1 — Both figures enclose identical areas: \(\tfrac{1}{2}T v_f = T\bar{v}\) with \(\bar{v} = v_f/2\). Since area under the \(v\)–\(t\) curve equals displacement, both motions cover the same distance in time \(T\).

Oresme's insight was to see the theorem as a geometric fact about areas, not an algebraic identity. That reframing made a purely verbal scholastic result visible — and in a sense, obvious.

A6   The Hump and the Notch

There is a companion visual argument — arguably even more elegant — that shows why the triangle and rectangle have equal areas without computing either one.

Setting up the comparison

Draw the \(v\)–\(t\) diagram for uniform acceleration from rest: a diagonal line from \((0,\,0)\) to \((T,\,v_f)\). Now draw a horizontal dashed line at the mean speed \(\bar{v} = v_f/2\). This line, together with the two axes, outlines the "mean speed rectangle" \([0,T]\times[0,\bar{v}]\). Relative to that rectangle, the velocity curve creates two regions:

  • The hump — in the right half of the diagram (\(t \in [T/2,\,T]\)), the velocity exceeds the mean speed. The curve rises above the top of the rectangle, forming a triangular hump.
  • The notch — in the left half (\(t \in [0,\,T/2]\)), the velocity is still climbing toward \(\bar{v}\). The mean speed rectangle extends to height \(\bar{v}\), but the curve has not reached it yet. The empty triangular space above the curve and below the rectangle's top edge is the notch.

The congruence

Both the hump and the notch are right triangles with the same two legs: horizontal leg \(= T/2\) and vertical leg \(= v_f/2\). They are congruent. The hump can be slid left and flipped to fill the notch exactly. The area of the full velocity triangle therefore equals the area of the mean speed rectangle. The mean speed theorem follows without a single calculation.

Figure A6.1 — The hump (red) and notch (green) are congruent right triangles, each with legs \(T/2\) and \(v_f/2\). The hump exactly fills the notch, proving that the triangle area equals the mean-speed rectangle area without any arithmetic.

The general case. When the motion does not start from rest — with initial speed \(v_0 \neq 0\) — the \(v\)–\(t\) figure is a trapezoid. The mean speed line at \(\bar{v} = (v_0+v_f)/2\) again creates a hump and a notch that are congruent right triangles (both with legs \(T/2\) and \((v_f - v_0)/2\)). The theorem holds for any linear (constant-acceleration) velocity profile.

A7   Modern Translation

Oresme's geometric language translates directly into the calculus vocabulary of Lesson 2. Let the object start from rest with constant acceleration \(a\), so \(v(t) = at\).

Displacement as an integral

\[ \Delta x = \int_0^T v(t)\,dt = \int_0^T at\,dt = \left[\frac{at^2}{2}\right]_0^T = \frac{1}{2}aT^2 \]

Displacement as triangle area

The \(v\)–\(t\) graph from \(t=0\) to \(t=T\) is a right triangle with base \(T\) and height \(v_f = aT\):

\[ \Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\text{base}\cdot\text{height} = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot T \cdot aT = \frac{1}{2}aT^2 \]

Displacement as mean speed × time

The mean velocity is \(\bar{v} = (0 + aT)/2 = aT/2\):

\[ \Delta x = \bar{v}\cdot T = \frac{aT}{2}\cdot T = \frac{1}{2}aT^2 \]

All three derivations give \(\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}aT^2\). The integral is a machine; the triangle area is Oresme's picture; the mean speed is Heytesbury's theorem. They are the same fact, written in three different languages.

The area connection, restated. The area under the \(v\)–\(t\) curve equals displacement. For constant acceleration from rest, the curve is a straight line and the area is a triangle. The formula \(\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\text{base}\cdot\text{height}\) immediately gives \(\tfrac{1}{2}aT^2\) — no integration needed. Oresme's diagram is a proof by picture.

A8   Deriving \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\)

Worked Example A8.1 — Three Methods, One Answer

A car accelerates uniformly from rest to \(v_f = 24\ \mathrm{m/s}\) in \(T = 6\ \mathrm{s}\). Find the distance covered using all three methods.

1 Acceleration: \(a = v_f/T = 24/6 = 4\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\).
2 Formula: \(x = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(4)(36) = 72\ \mathrm{m}\).
3 Triangle area: base \(= 6\ \mathrm{s}\), height \(= 24\ \mathrm{m/s}\); area \(= \tfrac{1}{2}(6)(24) = 72\ \mathrm{m}\). ✓
4 Mean speed: \(\bar{v} = 24/2 = 12\ \mathrm{m/s}\); distance \(= 12 \times 6 = 72\ \mathrm{m}\). ✓

The general case: non-zero initial velocity

When the object begins with velocity \(v_0 \neq 0\), the \(v\)–\(t\) figure is a trapezoid. The trapezoid area formula gives displacement directly:

Figure A8.1 — For a body starting at \(v_0\) and accelerating uniformly to \(v_f\) over time \(T\), the displacement equals the trapezoid area \(= \bar{v}\cdot T = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0+v_f)\cdot T\). This is kinematic equation (4) from Lesson 2.

\[ \Delta x = \frac{1}{2}(v_0 + v_f)\,T = \bar{v}\cdot T \tag{kinematic eq.\ 4} \]

Substituting \(v_f = v_0 + aT\):

\[ \Delta x = \frac{1}{2}(v_0 + v_0 + aT)\,T = v_0 T + \frac{1}{2}aT^2 \tag{kinematic eq.\ 2} \]

The two most important constant-acceleration formulas emerge directly from computing the area of a velocity–time trapezoid — exactly as Oresme would have done it, 700 years ago.

A9   Historical Legacy

The mean speed theorem was proved in Oxford around 1335 — more than three centuries before Isaac Newton published the Principia (1687) and nearly three centuries before Galileo published the Two New Sciences (1638). The following timeline sketches the chain of transmission:

  • c. 1335: Heytesbury states and proves the mean speed theorem verbally at Merton College.
  • c. 1350: Oresme provides the geometric proof at Paris via the latitudo formae diagram.
  • c. 1380–1430: The Merton results spread to Italian and German universities through traveling scholars.
  • 1555: Domingo de Soto, in his commentary on Aristotle's Physics, explicitly applies the mean speed theorem to free fall — the first written claim that freely falling bodies undergo uniformiter difformis motion.
  • c. 1604–1638: Galileo derives and experimentally confirms \(x \propto t^2\) for free fall and inclined planes. He was almost certainly aware of the medieval tradition through de Soto and his own extensive reading.
What Galileo actually contributed. Galileo did not prove the mean speed theorem — that was already done. His contributions were: (1) identifying free fall as a physical instance of uniform acceleration; (2) designing experiments (the inclined plane) to verify this identification; and (3) connecting the kinematics to dynamics — the causal story of why bodies fall as they do. The mathematics was medieval; the experimental confirmation and physical interpretation were Galilean. For Galileo's experimental methods and the law of odd numbers, see Side Quest B.

What was new about the medieval approach

The most remarkable feature of the Merton–Oresme achievement is what it does not require:

  • calculus (invented three centuries later by Newton and Leibniz);
  • a correct theory of force (Aristotle's dynamics was wrong; the Calculators bracketed it);
  • any specific physical system — the theorem applies to any uniformly difform motion.

The Calculators thereby took a crucial step in the history of science: the separation of the description of motion (kinematics) from the explanation of motion (dynamics). That separation, which we now take for granted, was radical in the fourteenth century.

The limit of the medieval achievement. The Merton–Oresme result was mathematical and abstract. The Calculators did not claim — or check — whether any physical bodies actually move in a uniformly difform manner. It took two more centuries and Galileo's inclined-plane experiments to establish that freely falling objects near Earth's surface actually do follow this mathematical law.

A medieval theorem in a modern course

Every time you use \(v = v_0 + at\), \(x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2\), or the equivalent trapezoid formula, you are using mathematics first worked out at Merton College and the Collège de Navarre in the mid-fourteenth century. The \(v\)–\(t\) graph you drew in Lesson 2 is, in concept if not in notation, the latitudo formae of Nicole Oresme. Physics has a long memory.

Glossary

Mean Speed Theorem
A body in uniformly accelerating motion (from \(v_0\) to \(v_f\) over time \(T\)) travels the same distance as a body moving at constant speed \(\bar{v} = (v_0 + v_f)/2\) for the same time: \(\Delta x = \bar{v}\cdot T\). Proved at Merton College c. 1335; given geometric form by Oresme c. 1350.
Uniformly difform (uniformiter difformis)
Medieval Latin for motion in which velocity changes at a constant rate — equivalent to constant acceleration. The "uniformly" describes the rate of change, not the velocity itself.
Latitude of forms (latitudo formarum)
Oresme's graphical method: draw a quality as a vertical height above a horizontal time axis. Applied to velocity, the result is a \(v\)–\(t\) diagram whose enclosed area represents displacement.
Degree of velocity (gradus velocitatis)
The instantaneous velocity at a given moment — the medieval equivalent of \(v(t)\).
Mean degree (medium gradum)
The arithmetic mean of initial and final velocity: \(\bar{v} = (v_0 + v_f)/2\). The "mean speed" in the mean speed theorem.
Oxford Calculators (Merton School)
A group of fourteenth-century scholars at Merton College, Oxford — including Heytesbury, Swineshead, Dumbleton, and Bradwardine — who applied mathematics to the analysis of qualities and motions within the Aristotelian framework.
