1   Overview & Motivation

Imagine pushing a heavy box across a warehouse floor. You lean into the handle and shove — but notice that not all of your effort actually moves the box forward. If you push straight ahead, every bit of your force goes into forward motion. If you push at a downward angle, some of your force presses the box into the floor rather than sliding it across it. And if you push sideways — perpendicular to the direction the box needs to travel — you do absolutely no useful work at all.

The question "how much of this force is actually doing useful work?" is answered by a single mathematical tool: the dot product.

What you will learn in this lesson: How to calculate the dot product of two vectors, what it measures geometrically, why the angle between the vectors is the key ingredient, and how it underlies the physics definition of work. We also take a brief first look at the cross product — the dot product's sibling — to set the stage for Lesson 2.

Dot products appear far beyond physics classrooms. Computer-graphics engines use them to calculate how brightly a surface is lit. Recommendation algorithms use them to measure how "similar" two users' taste profiles are. Machine-learning models use them inside every matrix multiplication. Once you understand the dot product, you will see it everywhere.

2   A Quick Vector Review

Before defining the dot product we need a shared vocabulary for vectors. If you feel confident here, skim quickly; if anything is unfamiliar, read carefully.

Scalars vs. Vectors

A scalar is a plain number — it has a size (magnitude) but no direction. Temperature, mass, and time are scalars. A vector has both a magnitude and a direction. Velocity, displacement, and force are vectors.

Notation

We write vectors in bold — a — or with an arrow over the letter: \(\vec{a}\). In component form a 2-D vector lists its horizontal and vertical parts:

\[\vec{a} = (a_1,\, a_2) \quad \text{or} \quad \vec{a} = a_1\,\hat{\imath} + a_2\,\hat{\jmath}\]

The symbols \(\hat{\imath}\) and \(\hat{\jmath}\) are unit vectors pointing along the positive x-axis and y-axis respectively. In three dimensions we add a third component \(a_3\) along the \(\hat{k}\) (z) axis.

Magnitude

The magnitude of a vector is its length, written \(|\vec{a}|\). In 2-D it is the Pythagorean distance:

\[|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2}\]

In 3-D: \(|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}\). Magnitude is always a non-negative scalar.

3   Defining the Dot Product

The dot product of two vectors \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) is written \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}\) (read "a dot b"). There are two completely equivalent ways to define it.

Definition A — Algebraic (component) form

Multiply the matching components together, then add all the products:

\[\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 \qquad \text{(2-D)}\]
\[\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 \qquad \text{(3-D)}\]

Notice the result is a single number — a scalar. There is no direction attached to it. This is a crucial feature: the dot product turns two vectors into a scalar.

Definition B — Geometric (angle) form

Place both vectors tail-to-tail and let \(\theta\) be the angle between them (always between 0° and 180°):

\[\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}|\cos\theta\]

The result is again a scalar. The two definitions are different expressions of the same quantity — they always give the same number. (The proof follows from the law of cosines and makes a great exercise.)

Key Insight: The dot product is the only standard vector operation that produces a scalar. Multiplying two vectors with \(\cdot\) collapses direction information down to a single number that captures how much the two vectors "agree" in direction.

4   The Geometric Intuition

Numbers are only useful if we understand what they mean. So what does a dot product of, say, 11 actually represent?

The Projection Interpretation

Imagine shining a spotlight straight down onto the line of \(\vec{a}\). The scalar projection of \(\vec{b}\) onto \(\vec{a}\) is the length of the shadow that \(\vec{b}\) casts onto \(\vec{a}\)'s line. That shadow length is \(|\vec{b}|\cos\theta\).

Then:

\[\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| \times \underbrace{|\vec{b}|\cos\theta}_{\text{shadow of }\vec{b}\text{ onto }\vec{a}}\]

Equivalently, you can think of it as the shadow of \(\vec{a}\) onto \(\vec{b}\) times \(|\vec{b}|\) — both interpretations give the same number. This symmetry is not a coincidence: the formula \(|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta\) is symmetric in \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) because \(\cos\theta\) is the same no matter which vector you measure the angle from.

Where does \(|\vec{b}|\cos\theta\) come from?

If \(\cos\theta\) appearing in the formula feels like it came out of nowhere, here is the step-by-step derivation from the right-triangle definition of cosine you already know. Drop a perpendicular from the tip of \(\vec{b}\) straight down to the line of \(\vec{a}\). This creates a right triangle with three clearly named sides:

  • Hypotenuse — the full length of \(\vec{b}\), which equals \(|\vec{b}|\)
  • Adjacent side — the leg that runs along \(\vec{a}\) from the origin to the foot of the perpendicular
  • Opposite side — the perpendicular drop from the tip of \(\vec{b}\) down to \(\vec{a}\)'s line

The angle at the origin of this triangle is \(\theta\) — exactly the angle between the two vectors.

The right triangle formed by dropping a perpendicular from the tip of \(\vec{b}\) to the line of \(\vec{a}\). The hypotenuse is \(|\vec{b}|\); the adjacent side is \(|\vec{b}|\cos\theta\) — the projection onto \(\vec{a}\).

Now apply the definition of cosine to this triangle:

\[\cos\theta \;=\; \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \;=\; \frac{\text{adjacent}}{|\vec{b}|}\]

Rearrange for the adjacent side:

\[\text{adjacent} \;=\; |\vec{b}|\cos\theta\]

That adjacent side is the projection — how far along \(\vec{a}\)'s direction the vector \(\vec{b}\) reaches. Multiply by the full length of \(\vec{a}\) and you have the dot product:

\[\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} \;=\; |\vec{a}| \;\times\; \underbrace{|\vec{b}|\cos\theta}_{\substack{\text{adjacent side} \\ \text{(projection)}}} \]
Key Insight: \(\cos\theta\) is doing one specific job: extracting the adjacent side of the right triangle, which is the component of \(\vec{b}\) that runs parallel to \(\vec{a}\). The opposite side — \(|\vec{b}|\sin\theta\) — is perpendicular to \(\vec{a}\) and contributes nothing to the alignment, so it disappears from the formula entirely. This is why the dot product uses \(\cos\theta\) and not \(\sin\theta\).

Figure 1 — The dot product equals the length of \(\vec{a}\) times the shadow (projection) of \(\vec{b}\) onto \(\vec{a}\)'s direction.

The Three Key Angles

The cosine function is the key. Because \(\cos\theta\) ranges from \(-1\) to \(1\), the dot product can be positive, zero, or negative depending entirely on \(\theta\).

0° < θ < 90° positive positive Vectors lean the same way — partial agreement
90° (perpendicular) 0 0 Vectors share no component along each other — zero agreement

180° (opposite directions) −1 \(-|\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}|\) (minimum) Vectors point in exactly opposite directions — maximum opposition

Key Insight: A positive dot product means the vectors lean the same way. Zero means they are at right angles. Negative means they lean in opposite directions. You can predict the sign of a dot product from a quick sketch — no arithmetic needed.

Why Both Definitions Give the Same Answer

This is not obvious — why should multiplying matching components give the same result as \(|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta\)? Here is a sketch proof using the law of cosines.

Place \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) tail-to-tail. The vector \(\vec{a} - \vec{b}\) forms the third side of a triangle, so the law of cosines gives:

\[|\vec{a} - \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2 - 2|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta\]

Now expand the left side using components \(\vec{a} = (a_1, a_2)\), \(\vec{b} = (b_1, b_2)\):

\[\begin{aligned} |\vec{a} - \vec{b}|^2 &= (a_1 - b_1)^2 + (a_2 - b_2)^2 \\ &= a_1^2 - 2a_1 b_1 + b_1^2 + a_2^2 - 2a_2 b_2 + b_2^2 \\ &= \underbrace{(a_1^2 + a_2^2)}_{|\vec{a}|^2} + \underbrace{(b_1^2 + b_2^2)}_{|\vec{b}|^2} - 2(a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2) \end{aligned}\]

Setting the two expressions for \(|\vec{a}-\vec{b}|^2\) equal and cancelling \(|\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2\):

\[-2(a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2) = -2|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta\]

Divide both sides by \(-2\):

\[a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta\]

The two definitions are exactly equal — not just approximately, but always.

5   Calculating the Dot Product

Pause and predict: Let \(\vec{a} = (1, 0)\) and \(\vec{b} = (0, 1)\). Without computing, what is \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}\)? These point along the x- and y-axes — what does Section 4 tell you about vectors at 90°? Check your answer in Example 3 below.

Both definitions lead to the same answer. Here are three worked examples showing both routes.

Example 1 — Component method (2-D)

Let \(\vec{a} = (3, 4)\) and \(\vec{b} = (1, 2)\). Find \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}\).

1. Multiply the first components: \(a_1 b_1 = 3 \times 1 = 3\)
2. Multiply the second components: \(a_2 b_2 = 4 \times 2 = 8\)
3. Add: \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 3 + 8 = \mathbf{11}\)

The answer is the scalar 11. Notice that we never produced a new vector — just a number.

Example 2 — Angle method

Two vectors have magnitudes \(|\vec{a}| = 5\) and \(|\vec{b}| = 6\), and the angle between them is \(\theta = 60°\). Find \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}\).

1. Write the formula: \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}|\cos\theta\)
2. Substitute: \(= 5 \times 6 \times \cos 60°\)
3. Evaluate \(\cos 60° = 0.5\): \(= 30 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{15}\)

Example 3 — Cross-check: perpendicular vectors

Let \(\vec{a} = (3, 0)\) and \(\vec{b} = (0, 4)\). These vectors point along the x- and y-axes respectively.

1. Component method: \(3 \times 0 + 0 \times 4 = 0\)
2. Angle method: The angle between the x-axis and y-axis is 90°. \(\cos 90° = 0\), so \(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = 3 \times 4 \times 0 = 0\) ✓

Both methods agree: the dot product is zero whenever two vectors are perpendicular. This will be important very soon.

Watch out: The dot product is not the same as ordinary multiplication of two scalars. You cannot write \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| \times |\vec{b}|\) — that would ignore the angle entirely and be wrong (unless \(\theta = 0°\)).

Example 4 — Three dimensions

Let \(\vec{a} = (1, 2, 3)\) and \(\vec{b} = (4, -1, 0)\). Find \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}\).

1. Multiply first components: \(1 \times 4 = 4\)
2. Multiply second components: \(2 \times (-1) = -2\)
3. Multiply third components: \(3 \times 0 = 0\)
4. Add: \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 4 + (-2) + 0 = \mathbf{2}\)

The result is positive, so the angle between these 3-D vectors is acute. The formula extends naturally: add one more product of matching components for each extra dimension.

6   From Geometry to Physics

The geometric picture from Section 4 has a direct physical consequence. Imagine you are walking north-east and the wind blows from due east. The wind only fights the eastward part of your motion — the northward part is completely unaffected. The dot product formalises this: it extracts exactly the component of one vector that runs along the direction of another.

Any vector \(\vec{b}\) can be split into two perpendicular parts relative to a direction \(\vec{a}\): a parallel component of length \(|\vec{b}|\cos\theta\) (along \(\vec{a}\)) and a perpendicular component of length \(|\vec{b}|\sin\theta\) (across \(\vec{a}\)). The dot product \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}\) sees only the parallel component; the perpendicular part contributes nothing because \(\cos 90° = 0\).

Key Insight: The dot product does one specific thing — it isolates the component of one vector that points in the direction of the other, then scales it by the other vector's length. Everything else vanishes.

This is precisely what physicists need when a force acts at an angle to a displacement. Only the force component along the direction of motion accelerates the object in that direction. The perpendicular component either presses the object into a surface (doing no horizontal work) or lifts it against gravity. The dot product automatically extracts the useful part — which is why Work = \(\vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}\).

7   Application: Work = Force · Displacement

The Physical Setup

In physics, work is the energy transferred to (or from) an object by a force acting over a displacement. Intuitively: how hard are you pushing, how far does the object move, and — crucially — are you pushing in the right direction?

Figure 3 — A force \(\vec{F}\) applied at angle \(\theta\) to the horizontal. Only the horizontal component \(F\cos\theta\) acts along the displacement \(\vec{d}\) — that is the part that does work.

The Definition of Work

\[W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = |\vec{F}|\,|\vec{d}|\cos\theta\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the force vector and the displacement vector

The unit of work is the joule (J) = newton × metre (N·m).

Why does this formula make physical sense? Because only the component of force along the direction of motion causes the object to accelerate in that direction. The perpendicular component either pushes the object into the surface (doing nothing useful horizontally) or lifts it (doing work against gravity, not along \(\vec{d}\)). The dot product automatically isolates the useful component.

Three Instructive Cases

Scenarioθcos θWorkInterpretation
Pushing a box straight forward 0° 1 \(W = Fd\) (maximum) All force contributes to motion
Carrying a bag horizontally (force is upward) 90° 0 \(W = 0\) Force and motion are perpendicular — zero work done on the bag
Friction on a sliding box 180° −1 \(W = -Fd\) Force opposes motion — friction removes kinetic energy
Everyday example: You can carry a full grocery bag all the way across a parking lot and do zero work on it (in the physics sense). Your upward force (supporting the bag) is perpendicular to your horizontal displacement, so \(\cos 90° = 0\) and \(W = 0\). Your muscles still tire — but that energy goes into keeping your arm contracted, not into the bag's motion.

Worked Problem

Pushing a Lawnmower

A person pushes a lawnmower handle with a force of 80 N at an angle of 35° below the horizontal. The mower travels 20 m horizontally. How much work does the person do on the mower?

0. Identify the angle between \(\vec{F}\) and \(\vec{d}\). The force is directed 35° below the horizontal; the displacement is horizontal. The angle between them is therefore \(\theta = 35°\). This step is easy to skip and often causes errors — always draw the two vectors tail-to-tail before naming \(\theta\).
1. Identify the magnitudes: \(|\vec{F}| = 80\text{ N}\), \(|\vec{d}| = 20\text{ m}\)
2. Apply the work formula: \(W = |\vec{F}|\,|\vec{d}|\cos\theta\)
3. Substitute: \(W = 80 \times 20 \times \cos 35°\)
4. Evaluate: \(\cos 35° \approx 0.819\), so \(W = 1600 \times 0.819 \approx \mathbf{1310\text{ J}}\)

If the person had pushed perfectly horizontally (θ = 0°), the work would be \(80 \times 20 = 1600\text{ J}\). The 35° angle reduces useful work by about 18%.

Negative Work

When \(\theta > 90°\), work is negative. This means the force is removing energy from the object. Brakes do negative work on a car — they apply a force opposing motion, converting kinetic energy into heat. Friction also does negative work on a sliding box, slowing it down. Negative work is not "bad" — it simply means the force is acting as an energy sink rather than a source.

8   Properties of the Dot Product

These algebraic rules hold for all vectors \(\vec{a}\), \(\vec{b}\), \(\vec{c}\) and any scalar \(k\):

Commutative

\[\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = \vec{b} \cdot \vec{a}\]

The angle between \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) is the same as the angle between \(\vec{b}\) and \(\vec{a}\), and \(\cos\theta\) is symmetric, so the order doesn't matter.

Distributive over addition

\[\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} + \vec{a} \cdot \vec{c}\]

Compatible with scalar multiplication

\[(k\vec{a}) \cdot \vec{b} = k\,(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})\]

Self-dot product gives magnitude squared

\[\vec{a} \cdot \vec{a} = |\vec{a}|^2\]

Since the angle between a vector and itself is 0°, and \(\cos 0° = 1\), we get \(|\vec{a}||\vec{a}| \times 1 = |\vec{a}|^2\). This is a useful shortcut: if you ever need to square a magnitude, use the dot product.

Not associative (in the usual sense)

\[(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}) \cdot \vec{c} \quad \text{is undefined}\]

\(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}\) is a scalar. You cannot take the dot product of a scalar with a vector. Associativity requires all three objects to be the same type, so it doesn't apply here.

Quick test for perpendicularity: Two vectors are perpendicular if and only if \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0\). This is one of the most-used tests in geometry and physics.

9   A Glimpse at the Cross Product

The dot product is one of two standard ways to "multiply" two vectors. The other is the cross product, written \(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}\).

Dot Product \(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}\)Cross Product \(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}\)
Result typeScalar (a number)Vector (has direction)
Uses angle via\(\cos\theta\)\(\sin\theta\)
Maximum whenVectors are parallel (θ = 0°)Vectors are perpendicular (θ = 90°)
Zero whenVectors are perpendicular (θ = 90°)Vectors are parallel (θ = 0° or 180°)
Direction of resultN/A (scalar)Perpendicular to both input vectors
Physics useWork, power, projectionTorque, angular momentum, magnetic force

Notice the beautiful symmetry: the dot product is zero when vectors are perpendicular, while the cross product is zero when they are parallel — they answer complementary questions. The dot product asks "how much do these vectors agree?" The cross product asks "how much do these vectors differ in direction?"

When you turn a wrench to tighten a bolt, the torque depends on how perpendicular your force is to the wrench — perpendicular force gives maximum torque. That is \(\sin\theta\) at work, not \(\cos\theta\). The cross product handles all such rotational situations.

10   Summary

Return to the question from Section 1: you push a heavy box across a warehouse floor. If your force is 100 N directed 35° above the horizontal and the box moves 5 m, only \(100\cos 35° \approx 82\text{ N}\) of your force contributes to horizontal motion. The work done is \(W = 100 \times 5 \times \cos 35° \approx 410\text{ J}\). The other \(\approx 18\text{ N}\) presses vertically into the floor and does no work on the box. That is the dot product in action.

  • Two definitions, one result: \(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta\). Always use whichever form is more convenient for the information you have.
  • The result is always a scalar. Direction information is compressed into a single signed number.
  • Sign tells you alignment: positive → vectors lean the same way; zero → perpendicular; negative → vectors lean in opposite directions.
  • Geometric meaning: the dot product measures how much of one vector's length falls along the other's direction (the scalar projection), scaled by the other's length.
  • Work: \(W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{d} = |\vec{F}||\vec{d}|\cos\theta\). Only the component of force along the displacement does work. Perpendicular force does zero work; opposing force does negative work.
  • The dot product vs. the cross product: dot → scalar, uses \(\cos\theta\); cross → vector, uses \(\sin\theta\). They are complementary tools. Lesson 2 covers the cross product in full.

Glossary

Vector
A quantity that has both a magnitude (size) and a direction. Written in bold (a) or with an arrow (\(\vec{a}\)). Examples: velocity, force, displacement.
Scalar
A quantity that has only a magnitude — no direction. Examples: temperature, mass, the dot product of two vectors.
Magnitude
The length (size) of a vector, always a non-negative number. Written \(|\vec{a}|\). In 2-D: \(|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2}\).
Dot product
An operation on two vectors that returns a scalar. Algebraically: sum of products of matching components. Geometrically: \(|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta\). Measures how much the vectors "agree" in direction.
Scalar projection
The signed length of the shadow that one vector casts onto another. The projection of \(\vec{b}\) onto \(\vec{a}\) is \(|\vec{b}|\cos\theta\).
Angle between vectors
The smallest angle \(\theta\) (between 0° and 180°) formed when both vectors are placed tail-to-tail. Determines the sign and magnitude of the dot product.
Unit vector
A vector with magnitude exactly 1, used to represent a pure direction. \(\hat{\imath}\), \(\hat{\jmath}\), \(\hat{k}\) point along the positive x, y, z axes.
Component
The portion of a vector along a specific axis. A scalar value. For \(\vec{a} = (a_1, a_2)\), \(a_1\) is the x-component and \(a_2\) the y-component.
Perpendicular
At a 90° angle to each other. Two vectors are perpendicular if and only if their dot product is zero.
Parallel
Pointing in the same direction (0°) or exactly opposite directions (180°). Parallel vectors have \(|\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\).
Work (physics)
The energy transferred to an object by a force acting over a displacement. \(W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{d}\), measured in joules (J). Positive work adds energy; negative work removes energy.
Displacement
A vector from an object's starting position to its ending position. Represents how far and in what direction the object moved.
Force
A push or pull acting on an object. A vector measured in newtons (N). Multiple forces can act simultaneously; their effects add as vectors.
Cross product
A second vector multiplication operation (\(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}\)) that produces a vector perpendicular to both inputs. Uses \(\sin\theta\) instead of \(\cos\theta\). Covered in full in Lesson 2.
